SAMPLE 01 · SOURCE-LINKED RESEARCH SUMMARY

Digital inclusion: access is only the first step

Purpose: A concise evidence brief for an education team, NGO, public programme or business planning digital-skills support.

Executive summary

Digital inclusion is not solved by connectivity alone. People also need affordable access, usable devices, relevant skills, safe practices and content suited to their circumstances. The World Bank’s Digital Progress and Trends Report 2023 describes uneven digital adoption between and within countries and highlights affordability, speed and usage gaps. UNESCO’s 2024 SDG 4 monitoring report also documents differences in basic digital activities and the ability to assess online information. Together, these sources suggest that programmes should measure meaningful use—not just the number of devices distributed or connections installed.

Evidence points

  • The World Bank reports that digital progress is uneven and that access, affordability, quality and use all matter to closing the digital divide. Source: World Bank, Digital Progress and Trends Report.
  • UNESCO’s 2024 SDG 4 monitoring page reports differences between income groups in basic computer-related activities and online-information reliability checks. These are cross-country indicators, not a prediction for any individual learner. Source: UNESCO GEM Report 2024 — Monitoring SDG 4.

Practical recommendations

  1. Baseline: ask intended users which tasks they need to perform, what devices/connectivity they have and what barriers they face.
  2. Teach by task: practise email attachments, secure account setup, finding reliable information and completing relevant service tasks.
  3. Measure outcomes: report completion rates on agreed tasks, accessibility barriers, user feedback and follow-up retention—not only attendance.
  4. Protect users: minimise personal data collection, explain consent and privacy, and provide non-digital alternatives where appropriate.

Limits and caveats

This short synthesis is based on the two sources linked above. It is not a systematic review, a country-specific estimate, or proof that a particular intervention will work. Before policy use, verify the latest source tables, country definitions, publication dates and local evidence.

SAMPLE 02 · WEBSITE / BLOG ARTICLE

Digital skills programmes should measure what people can actually do

Illustrative website article · intended for education providers, NGOs and digital-inclusion programmes.

A programme can distribute devices and provide internet access yet still leave people unable to complete the tasks that matter in daily life. Effective digital-skills support begins by asking a practical question: what should a learner be able to do safely and independently after the programme?

Start with a small set of real tasks. Depending on the audience, these might include sending a document by email, recognising a suspicious message, checking whether a claim has a credible source, joining an online lesson or completing a public-service form. Select tasks with learners and test them in accessible, low-pressure conditions.

Next, design practice around those tasks. Explain the steps in plain language, allow learners to repeat them, and make space for different devices, languages, disabilities and levels of confidence. Avoid treating a single test score as a complete picture of a person’s ability.

Finally, report results transparently. Record the starting point, the proportion of learners who complete each agreed task, common barriers and feedback after a suitable interval. Separate observed results from assumptions, and do not claim that a programme caused wider social or economic changes unless the evaluation design supports that conclusion.

Research from the World Bank and UNESCO underscores why both access and meaningful skills deserve attention. The right measures depend on the population, context and programme objective; no single metric fits every setting.

Suggested call to action: Before launching a digital-skills programme, define three to five practical tasks, establish a baseline and decide how progress will be measured.
