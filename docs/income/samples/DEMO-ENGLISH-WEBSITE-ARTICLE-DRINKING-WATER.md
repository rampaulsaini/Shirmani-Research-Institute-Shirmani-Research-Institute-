## Safe Drinking Water: Why Access, Reliability and Quality Must Be Measured Separately

A tap-water connection is an important step toward better access to drinking water. But a connection alone cannot answer every question that matters to a household: Is water available when needed? Is it safe to drink? Are interruptions reported and resolved? A useful assessment must distinguish access, reliability and water quality instead of treating them as one outcome.

### 1. Start with what the data actually measures
India’s Ministry of Jal Shakti reported through the Press Information Bureau that, as of 3 March 2026, around 15.82 crore of approximately 19.36 crore rural households were reported to have tap-water supply connections under the Jal Jeevan Mission—about 81.71%. This is a dated, government-reported coverage figure. It should not be described as proof that every connection supplies water continuously or that all supplied water meets safety requirements.

### 2. Use a wider definition of safe service
The World Health Organization describes safely managed drinking-water services in terms that include water being available on premises, available when needed, and free from faecal and priority chemical contamination. This wider definition shows why coverage data should be read alongside service reliability and water-quality monitoring.

### 3. Ask practical local questions
A village, school or community organisation can begin with four questions:
- How many households are reported to have a tap connection, and what is the date of the data?
- How often is water supplied, and how are interruptions recorded?
- What water-quality tests are performed, how recently, and by whom?
- Is there a clear route for residents to report problems and learn what action followed?

Answers should come from dated records, relevant public dashboards, responsible local authorities and—where appropriate—independent testing or fieldwork. A single headline number cannot answer all four questions.

### 4. Treat conservation as a shared responsibility
Rainwater harvesting, maintaining existing water structures and protecting local water bodies can contribute to water management when they are appropriate to local conditions and maintained over time. India’s Jal Shakti Abhiyan: Catch the Rain knowledge resources include guidance on rainwater harvesting and groundwater recharge. The correct intervention depends on local rainfall, geology, demand, water quality and maintenance capacity; no single method fits every place.

### A simple community scorecard
For a practical first review, record:
1. **Access:** reported household connections and the reporting date.
2. **Reliability:** supply schedule and documented interruptions.
3. **Quality:** test date, parameters tested, results and responsible authority.
4. **Response:** complaint route, response time and resolution record.
5. **Conservation:** local actions, maintenance responsibility and review date.

Keep observations separate from official statistics and label any missing information clearly. If evidence is incomplete, say so rather than filling the gap with an assumption.

### Conclusion
Good water reporting does more than count connections. It explains what the figures mean, what they do not establish, and what additional evidence is needed. By measuring access, reliability, quality and response separately, communities and institutions can ask clearer questions and make better-informed decisions.
