# Sample Research Summary — Improving Digital Literacy in Community Programs
**Portfolio sample | Shirmani Research Institute | English research-summary format**

> Illustrative writing sample only. This is not a completed literature review, a client deliverable, or an independently verified research finding.

## Purpose
A community digital-literacy program can help participants use online services more safely and evaluate information more carefully. A practical program should combine basic skills with repeatable habits rather than relying only on one-time demonstrations.

## Suggested program components
1. **Source evaluation:** teach participants to locate the original publisher, publication date, author or institution, and supporting evidence before sharing a claim.
2. **Account safety:** explain unique passwords, multi-factor authentication, phishing warning signs, and the risks of sharing one-time passcodes.
3. **Privacy awareness:** help participants identify sensitive information and review an application's permissions and data practices before use.
4. **Task-based practice:** use realistic exercises such as checking a public-service notice, identifying a suspicious message, and completing a mock online form without entering real personal data.
5. **Accessible delivery:** provide plain-language instructions, local-language explanations, large-text options, and practice time for participants with different levels of prior experience.

## Measurement plan
Before and after the program, use the same short task-based assessment. Track completion rates, correct source identification, recognition of phishing indicators, and participants' ability to explain why a source is trustworthy or uncertain. Report the sample size, task wording, scoring rules, missing responses, and limitations.

## Evidence and limitations
The recommendations above are a proposed program framework, not quantified proof of effectiveness. A real research assignment should add relevant primary sources, compare alternative approaches, record search dates, and distinguish measured outcomes from interpretation. No numerical impact claim should be made until suitable data have been collected and analyzed.

## Suggested next step
Pilot the lesson with a small consenting group, collect only necessary data, obtain appropriate permissions, and revise the material using observed task difficulties and participant feedback.
