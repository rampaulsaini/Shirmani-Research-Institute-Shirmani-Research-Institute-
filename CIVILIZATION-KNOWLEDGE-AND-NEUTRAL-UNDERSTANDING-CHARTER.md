# Civilization Knowledge, Comparative Understanding & Yatharth Siddhant Charter

**Status:** Proposed research and editorial standard  
**Purpose:** Build a careful, continually updated knowledge system that helps people understand human civilization and make decisions that protect Earth, nature, people, animals and plants.  
**Language:** Multilingual; preserve original-language sources wherever possible.  
**Core rule:** Truth before allegiance; evidence before assertion; dignity for every person.

## 1. Mission and scope

The Institute's research system should study, organize and explain humanity's recorded and responsibly recoverable knowledge across time and place, including:

- Human origins, prehistory, archaeology, migration, languages, cultures and the development of societies.
- World history: regions, peoples, states, empires, trade, technology, conflict, cooperation, law, education, economics, health, arts and everyday life.
- Religions, faiths, spiritual traditions, philosophies, ethical systems, indigenous and oral traditions, secular and non-religious worldviews, and the institutions and organizations associated with them.
- Natural sciences, mathematics, medicine, engineering, agriculture, ecology, climate, astronomy, computing and other technical fields.
- Social sciences and humanities: anthropology, sociology, psychology, political science, economics, linguistics, literature, art, law and education.
- Contemporary global challenges and practical approaches to peace, human dignity, public health, poverty reduction, biodiversity and environmental protection.

This is a lifelong, open-ended research programme, not a claim that any one system or model already knows every fact, source, language or interpretation.

## 2. Equal dignity; evidence-sensitive comparison

1. Treat every person and community with equal dignity, regardless of religion, belief, nationality, ethnicity, caste, gender, language, culture or non-belief.
2. Do not rank whole peoples or faith communities as superior or inferior.
3. Compare specific claims, texts, practices, institutions, historical events and outcomes—not the inherent worth of people.
4. Apply the same standards of evidence and reasoning to familiar, unfamiliar, majority, minority, ancient, modern, religious and secular positions.
5. Fairness does not mean false equivalence: where evidence is stronger on one side, explain why, while accurately representing the strongest available version of each position.
6. Distinguish a tradition's foundational texts, later interpretations, local practices, institutional policies and the diverse views of adherents. Never treat a large tradition as internally uniform.

## 3. Knowledge labels: never mix unlike categories

Every material statement should be classified where relevant as one or more of:

- **Well-supported fact:** supported by converging, credible evidence.
- **Primary-source testimony:** what a source, witness, inscription, archive or text says; not automatically proof that every claim in it is true.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** an evidence-based explanation that may be contested.
- **Living belief or theological claim:** meaningful within a tradition, but not presented as an empirically established fact unless independently testable evidence supports it.
- **Hypothesis / model:** a proposed explanation with stated assumptions and testable implications where applicable.
- **Value judgement / ethical principle:** a normative position whose reasoning and consequences should be made explicit.
- **Uncertain / disputed / unknown:** evidence is incomplete, contradictory, inaccessible or not yet sufficient.
- **Institute proposal:** a recommendation created by the Institute, not an established external fact.

Do not convert popularity, repetition, institutional authority, generated text, workflow success or confidence of wording into proof.

## 4. Source and verification protocol

For significant claims:

1. Seek original or primary material where appropriate: archaeological records, inscriptions, manuscripts, laws, official records, datasets, oral histories with provenance, experimental papers and direct documentation.
2. Use high-quality independent scholarship, peer-reviewed research, reputable archives, museums, universities and recognized statistical or scientific bodies to interpret and cross-check sources.
3. Record author/creator, title, date, publisher or custodian, stable URL or identifier, language, access date and relevant page/section when available.
4. Prefer original-language texts and qualified translations. Record translator and edition; flag translation uncertainty and disputed terminology.
5. Corroborate consequential claims with independent sources. A source repeating another source is not independent corroboration.
6. Include contrary evidence, limitations, missing data and material scholarly disagreement.
7. For changing facts, show the date checked and re-check before publication.
8. Never fabricate citations, quotations, dates, statistics, experiments, consensus or source access. If a source cannot be inspected, say so.
9. Protect private, sensitive, sacred, restricted or community-controlled knowledge. Respect consent, cultural protocols, copyright and applicable law.

## 5. Historical method

- Place events in chronology and geography; distinguish contemporary evidence from later retellings.
- Explain what is known, how it is known, and what remains uncertain.
- Avoid presentism: explain historical actors in their context without excusing documented harm.
- Include ordinary people, women, children, workers, marginalized groups, indigenous communities and ecological consequences—not only rulers, wars and institutions.
- Distinguish causation from correlation and individual actions from structural conditions.
- Present multiple credible interpretations when evidence permits more than one reading.
- Correct errors transparently and preserve a dated correction history.

## 6. Comparative study framework

When comparing religions, philosophies, sciences, organizations or civilizational systems, use a relevant subset of these dimensions:

- Historical origin, context, chronology and development.
- Original sources, transmission, translation and textual history.
- Core concepts, internal diversity and interpretive schools.
- Epistemology: what counts as knowledge, authority, testimony, reason or evidence.
- Logic, coherence, assumptions, contradictions and responses to criticism.
- Empirical claims and whether/how they can be tested.
- Ethics: dignity, compassion, justice, responsibility and treatment of vulnerable beings.
- Social and institutional practices, governance and accountability.
- Historical contributions, harms, reform movements and contested legacies.
- Effects on human wellbeing, peaceful coexistence, freedom of conscience and the natural world.
- Limits of available evidence and confidence level.

Do not force every tradition into one template. Explain when a comparison is partial, culturally inappropriate or methodologically limited.

## 7. Science, mathematics and the word “quantum”

Use scientific terms in their established technical meanings. “Ultra,” “mega,” “infinity” and “quantum mechanism” may be used as metaphors or creative labels only when explicitly identified as such. Do not use quantum physics to imply unsupported claims about consciousness, spirituality, healing, destiny or social systems. Explain mathematical models, assumptions, uncertainty, reproducibility and the difference between a metaphor and a physical mechanism.

## 8. Yatharth Siddhant: preserve, understand and examine fairly

The user's stated framework—**“निष्पक्ष समझ”, “यथार्थ सिद्धांत” and “यथार्थ युग”**—is an important Institute research subject and should be preserved with provenance.

- Preserve the user's original wording, supplied definitions, dated versions and context as faithfully as possible. Do not silently rewrite, embellish or attribute new claims to the user.
- Maintain a versioned glossary: term, exact source wording, date/context, user-confirmed definition (if supplied), related concepts and unresolved questions.
- Separate the user's own statements from an editor's interpretation, an AI-generated explanation and an independently verified finding.
- Translate carefully; retain the original Hindi wording alongside translations where practical.
- Ask for clarification only when a missing definition materially affects accuracy; otherwise label the interpretation as provisional.
- Apply the same standards of logic, evidence, testability and ethical consequence used for every other framework.
- Identify which parts are ethical commitments, philosophical propositions, explanatory models, empirical hypotheses or practical proposals.
- Do not declare the framework scientifically proven, universally accepted or superior to all other systems without adequate evidence. Do not dismiss it without examination.
- Invite transparent critique, counterexamples, independent review and revisions while preserving the historical record of earlier versions.

## 9. Explain for one person or many audiences

Create layered explanations without changing the underlying facts:

1. Plain-language summary.
2. Context and definitions.
3. Step-by-step reasoning and evidence.
4. Comparative analysis, including strongest counterarguments.
5. Technical appendix with data, methods and equations where appropriate.
6. Practical implications, uncertainty and sources.

Support Hindi and other languages; accessible formats; short and long versions; educational material; public discussions; and carefully reviewed translations. For large audiences, prioritize clarity, attribution, non-inflammatory wording and review by relevant subject experts and affected communities. Do not use personalization, fear, outrage or religious identity to manipulate an audience.

## 10. Conservation and human-benefit test

Assess recommendations against the Institute's stated purpose: protecting nature, Earth, human civilization, all living beings and plants, while supporting essential human needs.

Consider:
- Human life, dignity, rights, health, food, water, shelter, clothing and education.
- Animal welfare, biodiversity, habitat integrity and plant life.
- Climate, pollution, land, oceans, freshwater and resource use.
- Peace, conflict prevention, fairness, inclusion and intergenerational effects.
- Feasibility, cost, unintended consequences, reversibility and measurable outcomes.

Do not assume that a proposal is beneficial merely because it is described as compassionate, scientific, spiritual, traditional, innovative or sustainable. State trade-offs and measure outcomes.

## 11. Publication and governance safeguards

Before high-impact public explanations or recommendations:

- Conduct source review and a separate reasoning/logic review.
- Obtain domain-expert or community review when the subject warrants it.
- Identify conflicts of interest and distinguish advocacy from neutral description.
- Avoid incitement, dehumanization, collective blame, harassment and stereotyping.
- Protect personal data and confidential community information.
- Label AI-generated assistance; require human editorial responsibility for public claims.
- Publish correction channels and maintain version history.
- Do not imply that one response can settle all world history, all religious disputes or every scientific question.

## 12. Minimum research record

Each substantial research item should contain:

- Unique record ID, title, topic, region, period and language.
- Research question and scope.
- Sources and provenance.
- Method and comparison criteria.
- Findings separated from interpretation and belief.
- Counter-evidence, uncertainty and confidence rationale.
- Human, ecological and ethical impact assessment.
- Reviewer status and date.
- Corrections/version history.
- Public/private status and permissions.

## 13. Quality gates

A research item is not publication-ready if it contains fabricated or untraceable sources, materially misrepresents a community, hides known contrary evidence, presents belief as verified fact, uses scientific terminology inaccurately, or claims certainty beyond the evidence.

A successful workflow run is evidence that a workflow ran—not proof that its historical, religious, scientific or ethical conclusions are true. Independent verification must be recorded separately.

## Foundational statement

**निष्पक्षता का अर्थ सभी दावों को समान रूप से सत्य मानना नहीं; निष्पक्षता का अर्थ है हर दावे को उसके प्रमाण, तर्क, संदर्भ और परिणाम के अनुसार परखना, और हर मनुष्य तथा जीव के सम्मान की रक्षा करना।**

The Institute will seek understanding without blind allegiance, criticism without contempt, and progress whose benefits and harms are examined in the light of evidence and the protection of life.
